# The Twins and the Birthday Game

**Letter to Salvi — Phonic Pals Origin Story**
**Timothy Okoth-Osillo, May 2026**

---

I want to tell you something that happened on my daughters' seventh birthday, because it is the seed of everything I have been designing in the months since.

Hannah and Harmony are twins. And like almost every child their age growing up in Uganda today — children of educated parents, children who go to decent schools, children whose futures will be conducted largely in English — they were slowly, quietly, almost invisibly slipping away from Lusoga. Not dramatically. Not rebelliously. Just the gentle drift that happens when a language is never the language of any game worth playing, any song worth singing, any screen worth watching. Their Lusoga was the language of grandmothers and of instructions they already understood. Their English was the language of everything exciting.

I noticed it the way you notice a slow leak. And I noticed it in other people's children too. Some of my friends — educated, accomplished people — actually take pride in it. 'My child speaks only English.' As if losing the mother tongue is a certificate of arrival. I understand that impulse. We were schooled in exactly that logic. English was the door. Vernacular was what you left behind when you walked through it.

But here is what that logic quietly produces: the only people on the planet who speak Lusoga are us. There is no back-up population somewhere else keeping it alive. The same is true for Luganda, for Luo, for Runyankore, for every one of Uganda's forty-plus living languages. When we raise a generation that is embarrassed by those languages — or just indifferent to them — we are not choosing English over Lusoga. We are choosing the end of Lusoga. And we are doing it gently enough that no one raises an alarm.

> The only people on the planet who speak Lusoga are us. There is no back-up population somewhere else keeping it alive.

So. The seventh birthday. Hannah and Harmony's cousins from the village were there — kids who were more comfortable in Lusoga than in English, which made the usual children's games slightly awkward. And I was under that very specific pressure that fathers know well: the pressure to come up with something fun, right now, with what you have.

I split the children — and some of the adults — into two groups. English speakers on one side, Lusoga speakers on the other. The English side would call out an English word. The Lusoga side had to translate it. Then we reversed it. Lusoga word called out; English side had to find the equivalent. If you knew both, you were the MVP. If you only knew one, you were the learner.

Salvy — it was a banger. I am not using that word loosely. For the first time that afternoon, my daughters were leaning forward. They were competitive. They wanted to know the Lusoga word for 'umbrella,' not because I told them they should, but because they needed it to win. The cousins from the village were suddenly the experts in the room, and you could see them straighten up with it. Nobody felt behind. Everybody felt needed.

After the cake and the chaos, I sat down and I could not stop thinking about that game. About what it did. About whether it could be made bigger.

## Where I Was Coming From

I should give you some context for why my brain went immediately to technology when I started thinking about how to scale that game.

You know I am a lawyer by training and practice — Lead Counsel at Okoth-Osillo Advocates in Jinja, covering everything from land disputes to commercial matters. But over the past few years I have been quietly building technology products alongside the legal work, because I got frustrated with problems I kept watching go unsolved.

The first is **LegiClaw** — a legal practice management SaaS platform built specifically for Ugandan and East African law firms. Live at legiclaw.com. It covers case management, document automation, billing, client communication — the whole workflow that most law firms in this region still manage in notebooks and scattered WhatsApp messages.

Building it taught me how to take a profession-specific problem and engineer a solution that does not assume the user has access to expensive software, reliable internet, or an IT department.

The second is **Kayuwawa** — a property due diligence platform, live at kayuwawa.com. Uganda's property market is riddled with fraud — forged titles, disputed boundaries, ghost ownership, encumbrances that only appear after the sale. Kayuwawa lets buyers, investors, and field agents upload a title document and get an AI-powered risk report in minutes: verified ownership, encumbrance checks, historical flags, risk rating. What used to take weeks of manual searches and lawyer fees now takes minutes. The business model is novel for Uganda — agents and brokers use it as a trust signal with their clients, and buyers use it to protect themselves in a market where the land is often worth everything a family has.

Both of those tools were built on the same core insight: there are massive, well-understood problems in Uganda that technology can address affordably, if you design with the actual constraints of this environment in mind — not the constraints of San Francisco or London. That is the lens I applied when I started thinking about the language game.

## How Phonic Pals Was Born

The birthday game had a critical flaw: it needed me, or someone like me, to run it. The fun depended on a facilitator who knew both languages well enough to judge answers, keep score, and maintain the energy. You cannot put that in every school in Uganda.

But what if the AI was the judge for English — and the teacher was the judge for the mother tongue?

That was the unlock. A child speaks an English word into a microphone. An AI model running directly on the laptop — no internet, no cloud — gives instant star-rated feedback on pronunciation. One star, two stars, three stars. Objective, consistent, tireless. Then the same child speaks the Lusoga equivalent of the same word. The teacher — sitting on the side with a phone screen showing a simple confirm or reject button — taps yes or no. That recording is saved.

The game runs in teams. Buzzers. Time pressure. Stars on a big screen. Loud, competitive, joyful. The cousins from the village and the children from Kampala playing the same game, each group bringing something the other needs.

But behind the fun, something else is happening. Every confirmed recording is a verified data point. Over hundreds of sessions across many schools, a community builds a spoken corpus of its own language — the raw material needed to eventually train speech recognition, text-to-speech voices, and translation tools for Lusoga. The children who are playing the game are also, without knowing it, building the first digital voice for their language. The community owns that data. Nobody else.

I called it **Phonic Pals**. The hardware concept — a solar-powered, self-contained unit that can run the whole system off-grid in the most remote school in Uganda — I call the **Phonic Unit**. The full concept is documented. The software architecture is designed. I have been working through the details for months.

> The children who are playing the game are also building the first digital voice for their language. The community owns that data. Nobody else.

## Why I Am Writing This to You

When you shared the Place of Grace concept with me, I read it three times. Not because it was complicated — it is actually beautifully simple — but because I kept finding points where our two ideas were touching each other without quite overlapping yet.

Let me lay out what I see.

You are building a self-sustaining community on Gibwa Grace's land. Not just housing — a full ecosystem: a multipurpose centre with a library and classrooms, a childcare centre, a community kitchen, vocational training, a garden, intergenerational fellowship, shops, employment. A community that does not depend on the charity of outsiders for its daily functioning, that is organised around dignity and purpose and the long-term wellbeing of everyone on the land.

I am building a tool that, at its best, needs exactly that kind of community infrastructure to reach its potential. Not a school building, not an NGO's field office, not a government programme — but a place that already cares about the children, already has the elders, already understands that language and culture and identity are not decorative but structural. A place that owns what it builds.

The library and classroom in your multipurpose centre is where the Phonic Unit lives. The elder members of the community are the Cultural Umpires — the ones who confirm that the Lusoga word a child speaks is correct, who carry the knowledge that no algorithm has. The children in your childcare centre and nursery school are the players, the learners, the builders of the dataset. The vocational training programme is where young people learn to assemble and maintain Phonic Units — turning a community skill into a replicable model. The community-based organisation, Place of Grace, is the governance structure that owns the language data and decides what is done with it.

Neither of us designed it this way. But it fits.

> The elder members of the community are the Cultural Umpires — the ones who confirm that the Lusoga word a child speaks is correct, who carry the knowledge that no algorithm has.

Here is what excites me most about the combination: Place of Grace is already asking the deepest question, which is what does a community need in order to be genuinely self-reliant? Phonic Pals is asking a narrower version of the same question for language — what does a language community need in order to survive the digital age without depending on foreign institutions to represent it? These are not parallel questions. They are the same question at different scales.

A community that builds its own housing, feeds itself from its own garden, educates its own children, and generates its own income — and also owns the only verified digital archive of its mother tongue — is not just surviving. It is leading.

## What I Am Actually Proposing

I am not proposing that we merge our projects or complicate your planning with something new. The Place of Grace has enough moving parts right now, and the last thing it needs is a well-meaning idea attached to its side like a barnacle.

What I am proposing is a conversation — and maybe, after that conversation, a very deliberate alignment. Something like this:

1. The Place of Grace multipurpose centre allocates one hour per day, or a few sessions per week, for Phonic Pals sessions. In return, the community receives a Phonic Unit, a Lusoga word pack developed with the elders on the property, and full ownership of every piece of language data generated on that unit.

2. The vocational training programme includes a module on assembling and maintaining the Phonic Unit — essentially teaching low-cost solar electronics and hardware assembly. A young person who completes that module can build another Unit for another community. That is a skill with market value and a replicable impact.

3. The elders and older residents of the Place of Grace serve as Cultural Umpires — paid a modest session fee for their time, which becomes a small but dignified income stream from their own knowledge. Not charity. A transaction: they have something the game needs, and the game compensates them for it.

4. The library in the multipurpose centre becomes the archive — a physical and digital record of the language work generated on the property, maintained by the Place of Grace CBO.

5. Together, we build the story. The Place of Grace is a compelling visual narrative. Children playing a language game in a community built by their own people, alongside their grandparents, preserving a language that nobody else will preserve for them. That story is fundable. Internationally. From AI foundations, from cultural heritage organisations, from diaspora donors who feel the loss of language personally.

I know you have a lot on your plate right now with the Phase 1 construction, the legal documents, the family agreements. I am not asking you to start anything immediately. I am asking you to read this, sit with it, and tell me honestly whether you see what I see — or whether I am connecting dots that are not actually connected.

Because I am aware that I might be doing that. I am someone who builds things and who sometimes sees architectural alignments that turn out to be mostly in my own head. You are the person I trust to tell me which this is.

## One Last Thing

At that birthday party, when the game was at its loudest and my daughter Hannah — who speaks English with more confidence than I did at her age — leaned over and asked her village cousin how to say 'celebrate' in Lusoga, then turned around and shouted the answer before anyone else could... I thought: this is the thing. This is the exact thing. Not an education programme. Not a preservation project. A reason to want to know.

That is what I am trying to build. And I think, in a different register, it is what you are building too.

Call me when you have a moment.

— Timothy
